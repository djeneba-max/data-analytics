1. Start by reviewing the data.gov home page. What is this?

Data.gov is the official U.S. government website that provides public access to government datasets.



2. Go to Most Viewed Datasets. How many datasets are listed?

There are 584,200 datasets available on Data.gov.



3. Find the Topic Category for Human Health. Review the range of data sources
available for this topic. Why do you think these were tagged with the Human Health
category?

The Human Health category on Data.gov includes different types of data related to people's health, such as diseases, public health, air pollution, extreme heat, and food- and waterborne illnesses. 
I think these datasets were tagged with the Human Health category because they contain information related to people's health and well-being.



4. Go to the Food Price Outlook. Review the catalog page for this dataset. How can you
navigate to additional datasets from the USDA?

You can find additional USDA datasets by clicking on the Department of Agriculture organization link on the Food Price Outlook catalog page. This allows you to explore other datasets published by the USDA on Data.gov.



5. Go back to the data.gov home page. This time, click on Datasets by Organization.


6. Try searching for your city, county, and state. What organizations are returned?

I searched for East Orange, Essex County, and New Jersey on Data.gov, but no organizations appeared in the results.

7. Search for USDA, and click into the result. Within the USDA datasets, search for food
composition. Notice that the top three results are all datasets concerning nutrition
information. Try searching the USDA page for nutrition. How does that affect your
results?

When I searched for “nutrition” instead of “food composition,” the search results changed. The results were more focused on nutrition-related topics and datasets. This shows that using different keywords can affect the search results, even when the words are related.

a) One of the tricky things about working with data is that sometimes the terminology
used by a data source is not obvious. You may need to dig around and be creative
to find what you’re looking for.


8. Go back to the results for food composition, and click on FoodData Central. Review
the catalog page for this data source, then click on the button for Visit Page. Take
some time to explore the FoodData Central website.

a) Try searching for butter, or any of your favorite foods. What kind of results do you
get? What happens when you click on a result?

When I searched for butter, I found different types of butter and foods containing butter. When I clicked on a result, I could have access to more details about its nutritional composition. I even had access to data about samples, which I believe were used to determine the food's composition.

b) Go back to the home page. Find Foundation Foods, and click on Downloads. What
kind of data can you find on this page? How big are these files? Where can you find
more information on the data types available?

Foundation Foods contains data and metadata on individual samples of commodity/commodity-derived minimally processed foods with insights into variability.

The April 2026 dataset is available in JSON and CSV formats. The files range in size from 459 KB to 3.7 MB when zipped and from 6.5 MB to 32 MB when unzipped.

More information about the different data types available can be found on the FoodData Central Data Type Documentation page.
